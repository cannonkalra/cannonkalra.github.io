I've always been drawn to the space where hardware meets software — the layer where bits turn into signals, and signals turn into decisions. That curiosity is what eventually pulled me toward distributed systems and data platforms, back when I was first getting acquainted with tools like Spark and Storm.

Before data platforms became the focus, I was tinkering with Raspberry Pis, building small IoT networks over MQTT — hands-on work that taught me how unreliable networks and constrained devices actually behave outside a datasheet. That curiosity went deeper when I got into USB protocols and HID devices: I built a combined mouse-and-keyboard HID device from scratch, wrote its device descriptors, and got it running plug-and-play on ATMEL SAMD21 and STM32 boards. There's something satisfying about writing code that talks directly to silicon.

That hardware background turned out to be useful when I took on a client's fork of an open-source building-automation platform. The codebase had drifted from good object-oriented practice, and IoT polling suffered from starvation because there was no waiting queue. Firmware updates were also brittle — whole packages shipped over the air with no diffing, just checksums and rollback as a safety net. I rewrote the driver layer around factory and singleton patterns and introduced a proper waiting queue for the polling to fix the starvation problem.

From there I moved into building an industrial IoT platform from the ground up, joining a day-one startup as one of its first engineers. We piped data from PLCs, SCADAs, and ERP systems through MQTT into RabbitMQ and InfluxDB, tagged it with machine and business context, and turned it into dashboards that let plant managers compare performance across sites. I owned the pipeline end to end — from the driver polling the PLC to the published dashboard — ran proofs-of-concept with customers on two continents, and helped build the self-hosted deployment story, on Kubernetes and WireGuard, for customers who wanted the platform on their own infrastructure rather than in the cloud.

I then shifted into data engineering for a life-sciences company, splitting a monolithic data path into two purpose-built ones — search on OpenSearch, bulk dataset access on Delta tables — and scaling out genomics pipelines with Snakemake on AWS Batch. Moving that workload onto spot instances cut compute costs by 20% without slowing anything down.

I later returned to the IIoT platform for a second stretch, this time delivering a large-scale enrichment pipeline for a new customer and introducing Spark to handle the volume.

At some point I made the shift from full-time roles to independent consulting, and that's mostly how I've worked since — moving between clients, dropping into infrastructure that's usually under pressure in one way or another, and fixing it.

One client needed to get off a legacy container platform and onto GitOps; I converted their services to Helm charts and Kustomize and stood up ArgoCD to manage the whole thing declaratively. For the same team, I built an MQTT-based in-app chat system in Go and scaled it to handle 20,000 messages a second.

With another client, I turned a data lake into a proper lakehouse, introduced slowly-changing-dimension modeling, and cut cloud spend by roughly 20%. I built a Kinesis-based ingestion pipeline for next-day reporting and introduced Superset on top of Athena for BI, supporting dashboards over roughly 60 million events a day.

Security and cost efficiency have become a recurring theme in this kind of work. For one client I removed long-lived AWS keys in favor of proper service roles, set up a bastion host and WireGuard for private cluster access, and added VPC peering so services could talk to each other privately across regions. On the cost side, I cleared out stale data, upgraded their managed database, and moved workloads onto newer instance types — a 50% reduction in spend. I also stood up a self-hosted Spark pipeline feeding an OLAP store, handling around 200 TB of data a month.

Another engagement started as a rescue mission: a client's infrastructure had been destroyed, and I rebuilt their database and API services from the ground up, then hardened the setup and brought their cloud bill down by half. Their mobile app was stuck in a WebView, so I ported it to a native shell to give it proper access to phone APIs, and I chased down the API slowness that had been plaguing them — removing slow queries, fixing N+1 problems, and cleaning up the middleware sitting in the request path.

What ties all of this together, for me, is a preference for going in where things are actually breaking — under-provisioned pipelines, brittle firmware, infrastructure that fell over — and making them boring again: reliable, observable, and cheap to run. I like systems work that spans the full stack, from a device descriptor to a Kubernetes cluster to a BI dashboard, and I'm happiest when I can point at a number — a cost cut, a throughput figure, an incident that stopped happening — and say that's better now because of something I built.
